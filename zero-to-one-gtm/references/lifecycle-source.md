# A Zero-to-One GTM Development Lifecycle

## Executive overview

Yes—go-to-market can be managed with a lifecycle analogous to an SDLC. The important difference is that an SDLC turns known requirements into working software, while zero-to-one GTM is primarily a **search system**: the team converts assumptions about customers, value, pricing, channels, buying, onboarding, and retention into evidence. Steve Blank’s Customer Development model makes the same distinction: customer discovery and validation search for a model; customer creation and company building execute it.[^1][^2]

For a founder-led startup moving from pre-launch to its first 10 customers, the recommended lifecycle is:

**Frame → Discover → Design → Validate → Commit → Activate → Learn → Repeat**

This should run beside product development, not after it. HBS advises early-stage founders to ground GTM in the customer, the problem, and the customer’s learning and purchasing journey; it also emphasizes rapid, inexpensive qualitative discovery and postponing scale until the concept and model work. YC similarly advises teams to launch, talk to users, iterate, and do things that do not scale before expanding the team or acquisition machinery.[^3][^4]

The first 10 customers are not merely a revenue target. They are a deliberately selected evidence set used to answer five questions:

1. **Who has the problem most acutely?**
2. **What event makes action urgent?**
3. **What promise gets a response?**
4. **What commercial path produces real commitment?**
5. **Can the product deliver a result customers will retain, repeat, or recommend?**

Reaching 10 customers is therefore not automatically a pass. Ten unrelated buyers acquired through bespoke heroics may prove hustle, but not a coherent GTM motion. The lifecycle is complete when the team can describe a plausible, evidence-backed path to customer 11.

## SDLC and GTM

| Product-development concept | Zero-to-one GTM equivalent |
|---|---|
| User requirements | Customer/problem hypotheses |
| Architecture | GTM model: segment, offer, motion, channel, journey |
| Feature backlog | Assumption and experiment backlog |
| Build | Message, offer, campaign, sales, pilot, and onboarding experiments |
| QA/testing | Behavioral market evidence: replies, meetings, use, payment, retention |
| Release | Controlled launch to a narrow cohort |
| Production monitoring | Funnel, activation, customer health, churn, referrals |
| Incident review | Loss, churn, stalled-pilot, and failed-experiment review |
| Versioning | ICP, positioning, offer, price, and playbook versions |

A useful operating principle is: **standardize the learning process before standardizing the sales process**. Stage-Gate methods use gates as quality checks and investment decisions, but a startup should treat a failed gate as a prompt to loop back, narrow, or pivot—not as organizational failure. Strategyzer likewise recommends stating the riskiest hypotheses, defining tests and success criteria in advance, starting with cheap experiments, and updating assumptions from the evidence.[^5][^6][^7]

## The lifecycle

### Frame

**Objective:** Turn the founding idea into falsifiable GTM hypotheses.

Write a one-page **GTM thesis v0**:

- **Beachhead segment:** The narrowest plausible first market, described by observable attributes.
- **User, buyer, and influencer:** These may be the same person, but should not be assumed to be.
- **Painful job:** The progress the customer is trying to make and the current workflow.
- **Trigger:** The event or condition that makes the problem urgent now.
- **Alternative:** What the customer does today, including manual work, incumbents, or doing nothing.
- **Value hypothesis:** The measurable outcome the product might create.
- **Offer:** What is being sold now—design partnership, pilot, subscription, transaction, service-assisted outcome, or another commitment.
- **Motion:** Self-serve, founder-led sales, community-led, partner-led, marketplace seeding, or a hybrid.
- **Price hypothesis:** A real starting price or exchange of value.
- **Activation hypothesis:** The first observable customer outcome.

Every statement is an assumption, not a fact. Testing frameworks recommend separating desirability, feasibility, and viability risks, then prioritizing assumptions that are both essential and weakly evidenced.[^8]

**Core artifacts:** GTM thesis, assumptions map, evidence ledger, interview guide, target-customer screener.

**Gate — coherent test:** The team can name a specific customer, painful situation, current alternative, testable promise, and proposed commitment. If the target is “everyone” or the value is a feature description, remain in Frame.

### Discover

**Objective:** Establish that a recurring, consequential problem exists in a reachable group.

Founders should conduct structured conversations around actual workflows and past behavior rather than pitch the concept or ask whether someone “would use” it. HBS recommends interviews, observation, and ride-alongs early because they reveal depth that surveys often do not; it also advises watching behavior rather than relying on stated intent. Useful questions include:[^3]

- “Walk me through the last time this happened.”
- “What triggered it?”
- “How did you handle it?”
- “What did that cost in time, money, risk, or lost opportunity?”
- “What have you already tried?”
- “Who cared, who approved, and who did the work?”
- “Where did you look for an answer?”
- “What happened when you did nothing?”

Look for repeated evidence: the same workflow, trigger, pain language, workaround, consequence, and buyer pattern. Commitments of time, reputation, access, or money are stronger signals than compliments; Strategyzer distinguishes weak, stated evidence from stronger real-world behavior.[^9][^7]

**Core artifacts:** Interview notes, problem-pattern matrix, customer journey, vocabulary bank, initial account list, disconfirming-evidence log.

**Gate — problem evidence:** A narrow segment repeatedly exhibits the problem, can recount recent examples, already spends resources or accepts consequences, and is reachable through identifiable places or relationships. If evidence fragments across unrelated problems, narrow or change the segment.

### Design

**Objective:** Turn discovery evidence into a minimum viable GTM system.

Design only enough machinery to test the next major uncertainty:

- **ICP v1:** Segment, role, trigger, disqualifiers, current alternative, and ability to commit.
- **Positioning v1:** “For [specific customer] experiencing [trigger/problem], [offer] produces [outcome], unlike [alternative], because [credible distinction].”
- **Offer v1:** Scope, expected outcome, required customer effort, duration, price, risk reversal, and next step.
- **Journey v1:** Awareness → conversation or visit → qualification → evaluation → commitment → onboarding → first value → repeat/referral.
- **Channel plan:** One primary acquisition path and at most one secondary path.
- **Sales or conversion path:** Explicit stages with entry and exit conditions.
- **Activation plan:** The shortest path from commitment to first observable value.

The motion must fit the buying problem. A simple, low-risk product may support self-serve evaluation; a complex or high-stakes outcome usually needs a guided, founder-led motion. Bessemer recommends direct sales for complex solutions and self-serve or product-led motions for easy-to-use products, while still having founders land the earliest cohort.[^10]

**Core artifacts:** ICP card, positioning brief, offer sheet, landing page or concise deck, target list, outreach messages, discovery script, demo or prototype, onboarding checklist, CRM sheet.

**Gate — test readiness:** The team can identify prospects, make one unambiguous promise, ask for one concrete next step, deliver the proposed first value, and capture every result. Avoid building a broad brand system, marketing stack, content engine, or large campaign here.

### Validate

**Objective:** Test whether the ICP, message, offer, motion, and channel produce qualified demand.

Run founder-led, manually controlled experiments. Begin with warm network paths, then second-degree introductions, in-person settings or communities where customers already gather, and tightly targeted outbound. YC’s first-10 guidance says early customers commonly come through existing relationships and hands-on, unscalable effort rather than automation; founders should understand where target customers actually spend attention before choosing outreach channels. YC’s earlier first-customer playbook reduces the process to a list, relevant outreach, a meeting or demo, a price discussion, and a close—with founders qualifying customers themselves.[^11][^12]

Keep each experiment legible:

| Field | Example form |
|---|---|
| Hypothesis | Buyers with trigger X will prioritize outcome Y |
| Audience | A named micro-segment and list of people/accounts |
| Variable | Segment, trigger, promise, proof, offer, channel, or price |
| Test | A finite batch of personalized outreach or qualified visits |
| Pass signal | A predefined behavioral response or next-step rate |
| Strong evidence | Access, data, stakeholder introduction, signed terms, or payment |
| Decision | Persevere, modify one variable, narrow, pivot, or stop |

Do not change the audience, message, channel, and offer simultaneously. Use small batches, review the evidence, update the relevant version, and run the next test. Early discovery may justify learning thresholds rather than universal conversion benchmarks; arbitrary internet benchmarks are less useful than improving conversion within a consistent, qualified cohort.

**Core artifacts:** Experiment cards, outreach log, funnel by cohort, call notes, objections log, lost-reason taxonomy, weekly evidence review.

**Gate — demand evidence:** Multiple ICP-fit prospects independently take costly next steps, not merely calls. The team knows which trigger and message created interest, why prospects advanced or stalled, and which disqualifiers predict wasted effort.

### Commit

**Objective:** Convert interest into a meaningful exchange of value.

A “customer” should meet a defined commitment standard appropriate to the business model:

| Model | Valid early commitment |
|---|---|
| B2B or enterprise | Paid design partnership, paid pilot, signed order, or contract |
| Consumer subscription | Payment and meaningful product use beyond signup |
| Marketplace | A completed transaction; each side’s commitment tracked separately |
| Transactional product | Purchase followed by fulfillment or consumption |
| Freemium/product-led | Repeated core use plus a credible monetization event; a signup alone is not a customer |
| Regulated or long-cycle market | Paid feasibility work, procurement milestone, LOI with concrete obligations, or equivalent evidence—clearly labeled short of revenue |

Where pilots are appropriate, define scope, owner, timeline, success criteria, customer inputs, price expectations, and the conversion decision before work starts. Guidance on early pilots consistently warns against open-ended trials and recommends tying success criteria to business value, engaging the real stakeholders, and agreeing on what happens after the pilot. Sierra’s design-partner process used payment, a timeboxed relationship, shared accountability, and an explicit real launch rather than an indefinite prototype exercise.[^13][^14][^15]

Price is itself an experiment, but free usage often fails to test commercial demand. A discounted founding offer can be reasonable when the normal price, discount logic, duration, mutual obligations, and post-pilot decision are explicit.

**Core artifacts:** Proposal or checkout, pilot/design-partner agreement, pricing log, decision criteria, procurement map, commitment register.

**Gate — commercial evidence:** The buyer exchanges money or another deliberately defined costly commitment, the team knows what is being purchased and by whom, and there is an agreed path from evaluation to ongoing use.

### Activate

**Objective:** Deliver the first promised result, not merely close the deal.

Founders should manually onboard the initial cohort. First Round’s onboarding guidance argues that founders should directly onboard early customers even when the approach cannot scale, because the process improves activation and reveals what later needs to be systematized. For each customer, document:[^16]

- The outcome they believe they bought.
- The buyer, champion, operator, and any blocker.
- The specific first-value event.
- Required setup steps, owner, and date.
- The expected time to first value.
- A scheduled value review.
- Product, process, expectation, and customer-side friction.

“White glove” is useful when it teaches the team, but dangerous when it conceals that the product does not work. Separate product-delivered value from founder-delivered services in the evidence ledger. YC’s broader guidance supports manual, unscalable work early, while insisting that the purpose is to learn and iterate toward something customers want.[^17][^4]

**Core artifacts:** Mutual activation plan, onboarding checklist, customer health sheet, issue log, value record, support notes.

**Gate — value evidence:** The customer reaches the predefined first-value event, acknowledges the result, and demonstrates continued behavior consistent with the model—ongoing use, a second transaction, renewal intent backed by action, expansion, or a qualified referral.

### Learn

**Objective:** Turn 10 individual customer journeys into a transferable GTM playbook.

After every win, loss, stall, activation, and churn, update the evidence—not just the narrative. Review the cohort for concentration:

- Which segment, role, trigger, and use case recur?
- Which messages generated qualified responses?
- Which sources produced customers that activated, not merely leads?
- Which objections repeated, and which proved fatal?
- What was promised, and what actually created value?
- Which work was essential, and which was accidental customization?
- How long did each stage take?
- What did the customer pay, use, repeat, and refer?
- Can the same sequence plausibly acquire and activate customer 11?

The output is **GTM playbook v1**, not a growth department. Blank’s framework places scalable customer creation after discovery and validation, and YC advises teams not to scale headcount or product before they have something customers want.[^2][^4]

**Core artifacts:** Cohort review, win/loss analysis, source-to-activation funnel, ICP v2, positioning v2, offer/pricing v2, documented sales/conversion stages, onboarding v2, case evidence, next-10 plan.

**Gate — repeatability hypothesis:** A dominant pattern is visible across a meaningful subset of the 10 customers; the team can define who to target, how to reach them, why they buy, how they commit, and how they reach value. This is permission to test repeatability with the next cohort—not proof that the motion is ready for aggressive scale.

## Operating system

### Dual-track work

Run product and GTM as synchronized tracks:

| Product track | GTM track | Shared decision |
|---|---|---|
| Prototype a capability | Test whether the problem and outcome matter | Continue, narrow, or stop |
| Build minimum workflow | Recruit qualified design partners or first users | Which workflow deserves investment? |
| Instrument core action | Define first-value event | Does behavior indicate value? |
| Fix friction | Observe onboarding and objections | Product issue, message issue, or customer mismatch? |
| Release improvement | Retest offer and activation | Did the change alter customer behavior? |

This prevents the common handoff model in which product “finishes” and then marketing is asked to launch. Customer Development was explicitly designed to support ongoing product-development work, with deliverables at each stage.[^2]

### Weekly cadence

A lightweight founder cadence is sufficient:

- **Monday—Evidence review:** Update funnel, activation status, open assumptions, losses, and surprises.
- **Tuesday—Experiment design:** Select the riskiest assumption and predefine the audience, action, metric, threshold, duration, and decision rule.
- **Wednesday and Thursday—Customer work:** Interviews, outreach, selling, onboarding, observation, and support.
- **Friday—Synthesis:** Decide persevere, modify, narrow, pivot, or stop; update artifacts and product priorities.
- **Always—Customer follow-up:** Every promising interaction ends with a dated next step, and every activated customer is asked for relevant introductions after a demonstrated win.

Keep work visible on one board: **Assumptions → Ready to test → Running → Evidence collected → Decision → Playbook update**. Strategyzer recommends an experiment Kanban with explicit hypotheses, measures, success criteria, owners, and learning decisions.[^7]

### Evidence hierarchy

Use evidence strength to prevent optimism from becoming policy:

1. **Opinion:** “Interesting idea.”
2. **Reported pain:** A specific recent problem and consequence.
3. **Observed behavior:** Existing workaround, search, spend, or repeated workflow.
4. **Low-cost action:** Follow-up meeting, account creation, prototype use.
5. **Costly commitment:** Data access, team time, internal introduction, reputation.
6. **Commercial commitment:** Payment, signed order, completed transaction.
7. **Delivered value:** Activation and acknowledged outcome.
8. **Durable value:** Repeat use, renewal, expansion, or qualified referral.

Discovery legitimately begins with lighter evidence, but validation requires facts from real behavior rather than more opinions.[^9]

## Scorecard

At this stage, use customer-level evidence and cohort conversion rather than vanity metrics. Track counts and rates only where the denominator is a consistently defined, qualified group.

| Layer | Minimum questions |
|---|---|
| Discovery | How many qualified conversations? How many showed a recent instance, material consequence, active workaround, and common trigger? |
| Acquisition | Which source and message produced qualified responses and meetings? |
| Qualification | How many had the pain, urgency, authority or access, and ability to commit? |
| Conversion | How many reached proposal/checkout, committed, paid, or completed a transaction? Why did the rest stop? |
| Activation | How many reached first value, and how long did it take? |
| Retention | Who repeated the core behavior, transaction, or usage pattern? |
| Advocacy | Who supplied a testimonial, reference, or qualified introduction after receiving value? |
| Learning velocity | How many high-risk assumptions received decisive evidence this week? |

The most important view is a row per customer: segment, trigger, source, promise, offer, price, dates by stage, first-value event, time to value, retained behavior, referral, and learning. Aggregate dashboards can wait until the underlying definitions stop changing.

## First-10 sequence

A practical sequence is milestone-based rather than a rigid 30/60/90-day plan:

| Milestone | Primary work | Exit evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-launch | Frame hypotheses; recruit discovery participants; test workflow and language | Repeated acute problem in a reachable micro-segment |
| Prospects 1–3 | Warm network, second-degree introductions, manual prototype/service, founder onboarding | Costly commitments and first delivered outcomes |
| Customers 4–6 | Add targeted outbound or one native community/channel; tighten qualification and offer | Same trigger/message/offer begins to recur |
| Customers 7–10 | Retest the strongest motion with less founder improvisation; instrument activation and retention | Coherent cohort and credible next-10 hypothesis |
| After 10 | Run a repeatability test; remove accidental customization; document the playbook | Evidence that another operator or channel can reproduce parts of the motion |

YC’s current first-10 guidance recommends starting with the warm network and then meeting customers where they naturally gather, while maintaining personal follow-up. That advice is a starting order, not a universal law: consumer, marketplace, local, and regulated models may require different acquisition mechanics, but they still need behavioral evidence through the full journey.[^11]

## Decision rules

At each weekly review, make one of five explicit decisions:

- **Persevere:** The hypothesis gained stronger evidence; rerun with another comparable cohort.
- **Modify:** Keep the segment and problem, but change one element such as message, offer, price, or onboarding.
- **Narrow:** Focus on the subsegment or use case showing the strongest pull.
- **Pivot:** Replace a foundational hypothesis—customer, problem, value, channel, or model.
- **Stop:** Evidence repeatedly contradicts a critical assumption and no adjacent hypothesis justifies more investment.

A gate should answer: **What evidence changed, what decision follows, and what resource is now justified?** It should not ask whether the team completed a list of launch deliverables.

## Failure modes

- **Treating launch as the finish line:** Publicity does not prove that a reachable customer will commit and receive value.
- **Scaling weak evidence:** Ads, automation, agencies, and GTM hiring amplify an unclear ICP and offer rather than repair them.
- **Counting signups as customers:** Commitment must match the business model, and activation must follow it.
- **Interviewing while pitching:** Compliments and hypothetical intent contaminate discovery.
- **Running too many channels:** Small early samples cannot reveal what caused a result if every variable changes.
- **Free, open-ended pilots:** These create activity without urgency, commercial evidence, or a decision point.[^14]
- **Building for every request:** Early service is valuable when it reveals a common workflow; it is harmful when ten customers produce ten unrelated products.
- **Optimizing only acquisition:** A message can produce meetings while the product fails to activate or retain the buyer.
- **Hiring sales before learning sales:** Founder-led selling captures objections, language, buying dynamics, and implementation requirements that must inform the eventual playbook. Bessemer recommends founders land the first dozen customers and cautions against premature senior sales hiring.[^10]
- **Declaring repeatability at customer 10:** The first cohort yields a hypothesis for repeatability; the next cohort tests it.

## Discovery in practice: solving the cold-start problem

The Discover stage tells founders to have structured conversations about workflows and past behavior, but does not prescribe how to find participants when the founder has no existing audience, dislikes cold outreach, and wants to avoid anything resembling a sales pitch. The following method addresses that cold-start problem.

### The concentric-circles method

Think of the founder's network as concentric rings. Work outward one ring at a time; never begin with strangers.

**Ring 0 — The founder's own experience.** If the founder or team has personally encountered the problem, write down the workflow, triggers, consequences, and workarounds. This is the first "interview" and it calibrates every question that follows.

**Ring 1 — Existing relationships.** Former colleagues, friends in the industry, people from past companies who held the target role. The ask is explicit and low-pressure:

> *"I'm researching how [role] handles [specific workflow]. You've done this — can I ask you some questions about how it worked at [company]? 15–20 minutes, no pitch, just trying to understand the problem."*

This is not sales. It is a genuine request from a known contact. Most people say yes.

**Ring 2 — Second-degree introductions.** At the end of every Ring 1 conversation, ask:

> *"Who else do you know who deals with this regularly? I'd love to hear how they handle it differently."*

The Ring 1 contact becomes the warm introduction. One good conversation typically produces two to three Ring 2 leads. This is the single most powerful mechanism for building a participant list from nothing.

**Ring 3 — Communities where the target audience already gathers.** Find the places where the target role or segment discusses the problem without being prompted:

- Slack and Discord communities for the role or industry.
- Reddit subreddits, Hacker News threads, and Stack Overflow tags.
- LinkedIn groups with actual activity.
- Industry forums, professional associations, and local meetups.
- Open-source project communities, if relevant.

The rule is: **participate, do not pitch.** Answer questions, ask questions, and comment with substance. After consistent presence, people will engage because the founder is a peer, not a stranger. Discovery conversations emerge naturally.

**Ring 4 — Targeted outreach to strangers (warm by context).** Only after Rings 1–3 have produced sharp language and real patterns. At this point, outreach references specific, demonstrated knowledge:

> *"I've been talking to [role] at companies like [comparable companies] about [specific problem]. I noticed [pattern]. I'm trying to understand if this is common — would you be open to a 15-minute conversation?"*

This works because it is specific, references real knowledge, and requests a conversation rather than a demo.

### Reframing the founder's role

The discomfort with cold outreach usually stems from a correct instinct: most cold outreach is people pretending to care about a problem so they can pitch a solution. Discovery requires the opposite posture:

> **The founder is a researcher, not a seller. There is no solution yet — only a hypothesis about a problem. The goal is evidence that the hypothesis is wrong, because that saves months.**

Practical reframes for common interactions:

| Sales-oriented version | Research-oriented version |
|---|---|
| "Can I show you what we're building?" | "Can I ask how you handle [workflow] today?" |
| "Would you use a tool that does X?" | "Walk me through the last time [problem] happened." |
| "We have a solution for Y" | "I keep hearing that Y is painful — is that true for you?" |
| "Can I get 30 minutes?" | "15 minutes, no pitch — I'm just trying to understand the problem." |

In discovery, the best outcome is **disconfirming** the hypothesis, because that redirects effort before real resources are spent. The founder is not trying to convince anyone of anything; the founder is trying to find out whether reality matches the guess.

### A practical first week

1. **List 10 people already known** who are close to the problem space. Not prospects — people with relevant experience.
2. **Send 5 messages** using the research frame. Text, Slack DM, email — whatever is natural for that relationship.
3. **Join 2–3 communities** where the target role gathers. Lurk for a week. Note the language used, the problems raised, and the questions asked.
4. **After every conversation, ask for one introduction.** This starts the flywheel.

Most people are willing to talk about their problems when there is no sales pressure. Being asked about one's expertise is flattering, not intrusive.

## A/B testing in practice

A/B testing is referenced implicitly in the Validate stage (the experiment card structure supports it), but the mechanics deserve explicit treatment because the concept is often misapplied at early stages.

### Core mechanics

**Control (A):** The current best version — the page, headline, call to action, offer framing, or price that is already live. If nothing is live yet, the first version becomes the control by default.

**Variant (B):** A version with **one element changed**. One headline. One call to action. One price point. One layout. Not five changes simultaneously — one, so the cause of any difference is identifiable.

**Simultaneous traffic split:** Both versions run at the same time. Visitors are randomly assigned to A or B. This is critical. Running version A for a week and then version B the following week measures time-based effects (day of week, external events, traffic source shifts), not the variable being tested.

### The promotion cycle

The winner of each round becomes the new control. The loser is retired. A new challenger is designed against the surviving control:

| Round | Control | Challenger | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A (original) | B | B |
| 2 | B | C | B |
| 3 | B | D | D |
| 4 | D | E | … |

This is an iterative hill-climbing process. Each round tests one hypothesis. The control improves over time as winning changes accumulate.

### What to test first (priority order for early stage)

At the pre-product-market-fit stage, the founder is not optimizing button colors. The tests address **strategic questions**, ordered by impact:

1. **Who you are speaking to.** Two different audience or segment framings on the page: *"For DevOps teams…"* versus *"For platform engineers…"*
2. **Which problem you lead with.** Two different headlines emphasizing different pain points from discovery.
3. **What outcome you promise.** *"Cut deploy time by 50%"* versus *"Eliminate weekend incidents."*
4. **What action you request.** *"Book a call"* versus *"Start free trial"* versus *"Watch a 3-minute demo."*
5. **Price framing.** *"$99/month"* versus *"$999/year (save 17%)."*

### Practical early-stage implementation

At small sample sizes, full statistical A/B infrastructure is unnecessary. Workable approaches include:

- **Two landing pages with different URLs.** Share each in comparable but separate channels or audience segments. Compare response rates.
- **Sequential messaging tests.** Send 20 outreach messages with framing A, then 20 with framing B, to similar people. Not a true randomized A/B test, but sufficient for discovery-stage signal.
- **Simple tooling.** PostHog, Google Optimize successors, or two separate pages on Carrd, Framer, or similar builders with independent analytics.

### The right mental model for early-stage testing

A/B testing at this stage is not about statistical rigor with p-values and 10,000 visitors. It is about **structured learning with small samples**:

> *"I showed promise X to 15 qualified people and promise Y to 15 qualified people. X generated 4 meetings and Y generated 1. That is a signal worth acting on."*

The experiment card template in this document maps directly to this process: hypothesis, audience, single variable, pass signal, and decision. An A/B test is simply an experiment card where the single major variable has exactly two values, run against comparable audiences at the same time.

The danger at small scale is over-reading noise. A useful heuristic: if the difference is not large enough to be obvious without a calculator, the sample is probably too small to distinguish the two versions. Run another batch or test a bolder change.

## Adapting the lifecycle for builder-first founders

The main lifecycle presents a sequential path: discover the problem, then design the GTM system, then build. Some founders work differently. They synthesize pain and product naturally — they see a problem, understand it structurally, and build something that addresses it. This is a legitimate strength, but it creates a specific trap: building in isolation produces a finished product with no relationships to the people who need it, forcing the founder into exactly the cold-outreach, convincing-strangers mode that the Discover section is designed to prevent.

The fix is not to suppress the building instinct. It is to make building the mechanism that drives discovery rather than something that happens before it.

### Four parallel-path variants

**Variant 1: Build the smallest useful thing, then use it as a conversation starter.** Instead of building the full product, build a small artifact that addresses the pain — a tool, template, calculator, script, diagnostic, or checklist — something that can ship in days rather than months. Ship it for free. The people who find it and use it have self-identified as having the problem. The conversation that follows is not a pitch:

> *"You used [the thing]. I'm curious — what were you dealing with that led you to it? How did it fit into your workflow?"*

That is a discovery interview. It happened because the founder built something. The instinct to build funded the discovery process.

**Variant 2: Build with, not for.** Find two or three people who have the pain (using Rings 1–2 from the concentric-circles method) and propose a design partnership:

> *"I think [problem] is solvable. I want to build something with someone who has this problem every week. I'll do the building. You give me 30 minutes a week to show me your actual workflow and react to what I'm making. If it works, you get the tool and input into where it goes."*

The founder is not selling. The founder is collaborating. Every weekly session is a discovery conversation embedded inside a building process. At the end, there is a product, first customers, and the language to describe the value — all produced simultaneously.

**Variant 3: Build in public.** Share the thinking and building process where the target audience can see it. Write about the problem and why it is hard. Share design decisions and tradeoffs. Post the "why" more than the "what." This attracts people who recognize the pain. They arrive because they have the problem, not because they were pitched. The building is the marketing, and the marketing is discovery.

**Variant 4: Be customer zero.** If the founder has personally experienced the pain, build for personal use first. Get the tool to the point where it is genuinely useful in a real workflow. Then find communities where others have the same pain and share what was built and why:

> *"I had [problem], couldn't find anything that handled it well, so I built [thing]. It does X. Anyone else dealing with this?"*

### The common pattern

All four variants share the same structure: **build less, build connected to people, and use what is built to start conversations rather than to close deals.**

| Isolation path (the trap) | Connected path (the fix) |
|---|---|
| See pain → Build full product → Find strangers → Convince them | See pain → Build smallest useful thing → Attract or find people with the pain → Learn from them → Build the real product |
| Building and selling are separate phases | Building is the discovery mechanism |
| Launch day is the first customer contact | First customer contact happens in the first week |
| The founder must convince people the problem exists | People already know the problem exists; the founder offers a way through it |

The discipline this requires: do not build for months without looking up. Build the smallest thing, get it in front of people within one to two weeks, and treat every reaction as a discovery data point. If building has continued for more than two weeks without a single conversation with someone who has the pain, the founder has drifted back into the isolation path.

### Where this maps to the lifecycle

This approach is not skipping the Discover stage. It is running Discover and Build in parallel rather than in sequence. The dual-track table in the Operating System section already supports this — its first row reads: *Prototype a capability ↔ Test whether the problem and outcome matter → Continue, narrow, or stop.* The prototype is not something built after discovery. It is the thing built to drive discovery.

## Getting in front of people: a step-by-step playbook

"Getting in front of people" is the specific action that connects building to discovery. For a founder with deep pattern-recognition skills but limited experience in outreach, the challenge is not strategic — it is mechanical. The following sections provide repeatable processes that can be followed as-is and then adapted once the founder has enough reps to develop personal judgment.

### Step 1: Choose two to three places (once)

Before sharing anything, identify where the target audience already spends attention. The goal is to pick a small number of channels and commit to them rather than scatter effort across many.

**For technical audiences:**

| Channel type | Examples | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Forum or Q&A site | Hacker News, relevant subreddits, Stack Overflow, Indie Hackers | People are actively discussing problems; sharing a tool that addresses one is native behavior |
| Community chat | Slack or Discord communities for the role, industry, or technology | Smaller, higher-trust; repeated presence builds familiarity |
| Social/professional feed | Twitter/X, LinkedIn, Mastodon, Bluesky | Good for build-in-public; reaches second-degree connections |

**For non-technical or industry-specific audiences:**

| Channel type | Examples | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Industry forum or group | LinkedIn groups, Facebook groups, trade-association boards, niche forums | Members share the role and context; relevant content is welcome |
| In-person or hybrid | Local meetups, conferences, professional association events | Face-to-face builds trust faster than any online channel |
| Newsletter or publication | Industry newsletters, trade publications that accept contributor posts | Reaches a curated, relevant audience that already reads about the problem space |

**Selection criteria:** Pick channels where (a) the target role or segment is already present, (b) sharing useful things is normal behavior rather than spam, and (c) the founder can participate consistently — not just post once and disappear. Two channels maintained well outperform six channels used sporadically.

### Step 2: Share the artifact (repeatable process)

Each time something is ready to share — a tool, a write-up about the problem, a prototype, a template, a finding from a conversation — follow this sequence:

**Write the post.** A share post has three parts:

1. **The problem statement** (1–2 sentences). Name the specific pain in the language the audience uses. Do not describe the solution yet.
2. **What this is and why it exists** (2–3 sentences). Describe what was built and the decision or insight behind it. Be specific and honest. "I kept running into X, so I built Y" is more compelling than any feature list.
3. **The invitation** (1 sentence). Ask for something specific: feedback, reactions, whether others have the problem, how they handle it today.

**Example for a technical community post:**

> I kept hitting the same problem managing [specific workflow] — [specific pain point]. Couldn't find a tool that handled it the way I needed, so I built a small [thing] that does [core function].
>
> It's free / open source / available here: [link]
>
> Curious whether anyone else runs into this. How are you handling it today?

**Example for a LinkedIn or professional-network post:**

> [Role] teams spend [X hours / deal with Y problem] every [time period] because [reason]. I've been digging into why this keeps happening and built a [template / calculator / small tool] that [specific outcome].
>
> Here's the link if it's useful: [link]
>
> I'd love to hear how others approach this — what's working for you?

**What not to do:**

- Do not write a press release or launch announcement. This is a conversation starter, not a product launch.
- Do not describe every feature. Describe the problem and the outcome.
- Do not ask people to sign up, subscribe, or buy. Ask them to react, respond, or share their experience.

### Step 3: Respond and transition to conversations (every time)

When someone engages — a comment, a reply, a DM, a question — the goal is to transition from public interaction to a private discovery conversation. This is the critical step that turns attention into relationships.

**Response templates by engagement type:**

*Someone says "this is useful" or "nice work":*
> Thanks — curious what brought you to it. Were you dealing with [the problem] recently?

*Someone describes their version of the problem:*
> That's really similar to what I kept running into. Would you be up for a quick call? I'm trying to understand how different people handle this. 15 minutes, no agenda beyond learning how your workflow looks.

*Someone asks a feature question or requests a change:*
> Interesting — that tells me your workflow looks a bit different from what I assumed. Can I ask you a couple questions about how you're using it? Happy to jump on a quick call or trade messages here.

*Someone shares it or tags a colleague:*
> Appreciate the share. If [tagged person] ends up using it, I'd love to hear their take — always looking for different perspectives on this problem.

**The conversion goal:** Every public engagement should aim toward one of these outcomes, in order of preference:

1. A scheduled 15-minute conversation (the strongest outcome).
2. An async back-and-forth in DMs about their workflow (good).
3. A follow or connection that enables future contact (acceptable).
4. Nothing further (fine — not every interaction converts).

Do not force it. If someone just says "cool" and moves on, let them. The people with real pain will engage further because the problem is real to them.

### Step 4: Follow up (the step most people skip)

After every conversation or meaningful interaction, send a follow-up within 24 hours. This is where most people stop, and it is where the compounding begins.

**After a discovery call:**

> Thanks for the time today. The way you described [specific thing from the conversation] was really helpful — I hadn't thought about [specific insight]. If it's okay, I'd love to follow up in a couple weeks as I work on [related thing]. Also — you mentioned [colleague / team / company] deals with something similar. Would you be comfortable making an intro?

**After a useful DM exchange:**

> Really appreciate you walking me through that. Would you be open to a quick call sometime? I'd love to dig into [specific detail] a bit more. Totally informal — just trying to understand the problem better.

**The introduction request:** This is the flywheel. After every substantive conversation where the other person engaged meaningfully, ask:

> Who else do you know who deals with this? I'd love to hear how they handle it differently.

Not every person will provide an introduction. But every introduction that does come is a warm contact who arrives pre-qualified — they have the problem, and someone they trust told them to talk to the founder.

### Step 5: Repeat on a weekly cadence

Consistency matters more than volume. A sustainable weekly pattern:

| Day | Action | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Review the past week: who engaged, who responded, what language resonated, what fell flat. Update the evidence ledger. | 30 min |
| Tuesday | Prepare one thing to share: a tool update, a write-up, a question, a finding from a conversation. Write the post. | 45 min |
| Wednesday | Post in the two to three chosen channels. Respond to any open threads from previous posts. | 30 min |
| Thursday | Follow up with anyone from the past week: send thank-you messages, request introductions, schedule conversations. | 30 min |
| Friday | Have conversations (calls or async). Take notes. Ask for introductions at the end of each one. | 1–2 hours |

**Total time: roughly 3–4 hours per week**, leaving the rest for building. This is not a marketing job. It is a structured habit that feeds the building process with real information.

### What "working" looks like at each stage

Because this process is unfamiliar, it helps to know what normal progress looks like so that silence in week one does not feel like failure:

| Timeframe | Expected state | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Channels chosen. First artifact shared. Possibly no engagement. | — |
| Week 2 | A few reactions or comments. Maybe one DM conversation. | — |
| Weeks 3–4 | 2–5 real conversations (calls or substantive async). Starting to hear repeated language. | Zero engagement after 3 posts — reassess the channel or the problem framing. |
| Weeks 5–8 | 5–15 conversations total. Introductions starting to come from earlier contacts. Clearer sense of which framing resonates. | Conversations happen but no one describes real pain — the problem may be milder than assumed. |
| Weeks 8–12 | A small group of people who engage repeatedly. Enough pattern data to sharpen the ICP and offer. First design-partner or pilot conversations are natural, not forced. | Many conversations but no clustering — the audience may be too broad, or the problem may not be consequential enough to drive action. |

The first two weeks will feel slow. This is normal. The compounding effect of introductions and repeated presence is not visible until weeks four through six. The process is designed to be followed mechanically until then.

## Recommended definition of done

For pre-launch to first 10 customers, the lifecycle is “done” when all of the following are true:

- There is a narrow, observable ICP and a recurring trigger/problem pattern.
- At least one reachable channel reliably creates qualified conversations or product evaluations.
- Founders can describe and run the conversion path end to end.
- Ten customers have made the predefined commitment appropriate to the model; exceptions are labeled rather than blended into the count.
- A meaningful subset reached an explicit first-value event and showed follow-on behavior.
- Wins and losses cluster around understandable reasons.
- Product-delivered value is distinguishable from founder-provided manual service.
- ICP, positioning, offer, price, conversion stages, and onboarding are documented as versioned artifacts.
- The team can state why customer 11 should resemble the best of customers 1–10 and name the next test.

The resulting system is less like a one-time launch plan and more like CI/CD for commercial learning: small changes, controlled exposure, behavioral evidence, explicit gates, and continual updates to the product and GTM model. The goal is not to make early GTM bureaucratic. It is to make learning cumulative instead of anecdotal.
